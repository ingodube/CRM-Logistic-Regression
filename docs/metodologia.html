1 Introduction

Unlike the direct and explicit measurement variables commonly used in data science, complex phenomena such as skills and behaviors constitute latent variables, whose unobservable nature implies inherent subjectivity and measurement error. Originated in Spearman (1904)’s studies on human intelligence and founding the development of Factor Analysis, this concept defines such variables as theoretical constructs that, although not subject to direct observation, can be statistically estimated through observed indicators (Bollen, 1989).

The type of method used to model latent variables depends on whether the construct is on a continuous scale, or is divided into latent classes. Likewise, the nature of the indicators, whether continuous or categorical, also influences the choice of methodology used. One of the approaches used in the literature is Item Response Theory (IRT), which models the relationship between a latent trait on a continuous scale and the probability of an individual obtaining a certain result on a test (Hambleton et al., 1991). Despite the wide use of IRT in the psychometric or educational assessment context, IRT has a multidisciplinary character and can be applied in the most diverse areas of knowledge (Hays et al., 2007; Knoll and Houts, 2012; Hare and Poole, 2018).

The initial IRT models proposed by Lord (1952), Rasch (1960) and Birnbaum (1968) consider dichotomous items, that is, whether an individual responded correctly to a test item or not. Subsequently, Samejima (1969) extended the dichotomous approach to polytomous items with the Graded Response Models (GRM), modeling the probability of an individual responding to a given ordinal category of a test. From this, other approaches to categorical responses were formulated, such as the Model for Nominal Responses (Bock, 1972), the Gradual Scale Model (Andrich, 1978) and the Partial Credit Model (Masters, 1982; Muraki, 1992).

However, Samejima (1973) observed a need to incorporate responses on a continuous scale in the estimation of the latent variable by IRT. This is due to the fact that, in psychometric tests, continuous responses can be obtained in two ways. The first is by measuring the time until the completion of a certain activity (Roskam, 1997; Van der Linden, 2006; Ferrando and Lorenzo-Seva, 2007). The second way is when tests are answered using visual analogue scales in a straight line segment (Cella and Perry, 1986; Guyatt et al., 1987; Ferrando, 2001).

There is a third way to obtain normal continuous responses for measuring a latent trait, which is standardizing continuous indicator variables using the z-score method. In this case, variables that are measured on different scales are placed on the same scale to compare how many standard deviations a specific value is above or below the mean. This type of approach is very common in the construction of synthetic indices (OCDE, 2008), in which the use of IRT is applicable (Filmer and Scott, 2012; Vandemoortele, 2014).

In this way, Samejima (1973) formalized the Continuous Response Model (CRM) for responses limited to the range 0 and 1 and using the normal ogive distribution to model the latent trait. Wang and Zeng (1998) proposed a reparameterization of the Samejima model by applying a logarithmic transformation to continuous responses, still associated with the normal distribution. For the frequentist estimation of item parameters in the CRM, the authors developed a maximum marginal likelihood approach with the aid of the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (Dempster et al., 1977).

In many applications, however, the estimation of the latent variable is not the end point of the analysis. The estimated trait can be used as an explanatory variable in regression models to investigate its association with observed responses, denoted by distal outcomes. Schofield (2008) discusses that cognitive test scores, when used as predictors in regression models, carry measurement error that should not be ignored. However, when treating an estimated latent variable as if it were observed without error, the regression coefficients may become biased, with a tendency to attenuate the effect associated with the predictor measured with error (Junker et al., 2012; Schofield et al., 2015).

The literature on regression with factor scores and latent structure models also points out that stepwise procedures, in which predicted latent scores are subsequently treated as observed, can underestimate structural associations (Skrondal and Laake, 2001; Bolck et al., 2004; Devlieger et al., 2016). Although Schofield (2008) has emphasized applications in linear regression, the formulation itself based on measurement models and structural models allows extension to generalized linear models, including models for dichotomous responses. In this context, Bayesian approaches are particularly useful because they allow simultaneously estimating the measurement model and the regression model, propagating the uncertainty of the latent variable to the inference about the distal outcome (Fox and Glas, 2003; Junker et al., 2012).

Considering that, although there are proposals for modeling continuous responses in the context of IRT, this line of research remains relatively little explored when compared to the vast development of models for categorical data. Furthermore, the use of simultaneous equation models with confirmatory factor analysis as a measurement model is widely used in the literature (Kline, 1998). Despite this, continuous IRT models are perfectly comparable to factor analysis (Mellenbergh, 1994; Ferrando, 2002), offering other advantages, such as the interpretation of each item based on discrimination or difficulty parameters, as well as the evaluation of individuals’ response patterns (Ferrando, 1999, 2010).

Furthermore, there is a predominance of frequentist estimation approaches in the literature, both for categorical models (Hambleton et al., 1991) and for the recent developments of continuous models (Tutz and Jordan, 2024). In contrast, Bayesian formulations offer greater flexibility for modeling the measurement structure, incorporating uncertainty and extending more complex approaches (Fox, 2010).

Therefore, the general objective of this work is to use the latent variable estimated by IRT using continuous items as a predictor in a logistic regression model for a dichotomous distal outcome. Specifically, we seek to compare a two-step approach, in which the CRM is first estimated by maximum marginal likelihood with optimization via the EM algorithm and then the latent variable estimated as observed in logistic regression is treated, with a Bayesian approach of joint estimation of IRT and regression. Through a study with simulated data, the aim is to verify whether the joint Bayesian estimation corrects the underestimation of the effect of the latent variable on the distal response when compared to the two-step approach.

2 Methodology

The proposed model considers a single latent trait θi\theta_i, defined on a continuous scale for each individual i=1,2,…,ni = 1, 2, \ldots, n. Furthermore, let YijY_{ij} be the indicator variables observed with j=1,2,…,pj = 1, 2, \ldots, p, also defined on the continuous scale. In this context, the construct measurement model is defined in which the variations in the indicators are explained by the variation in the latent trait (Bollen, 1989).

Let WiW_i be an observed outcome variable, denoted by distal response, whose predictor is not directly observed. In this case, structural regression is defined, where WiW_i is conditionally modeled on θi\theta_i. 2.1 Item Response Theory for Continuous Responses

The proposed Measurement Model defined by CRM which is based on Samejima’s original formulation (1973, 1974). Considering a sample size nn and a test with pp items, let YijY_{ij} be the observed score of the ith individual in the jth item, such that 0≤Yij<kj0 \leq Y_{ij} < k_j, ∀i=1,2,…,n\forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n, j=1,2,…,pj = 1, 2, \ldots, p. Therefore, Wang and Zeng (1998) proposed the following transformation:

Zij=ln⁡(Yijkj−Yij)⇔Yij=kjexp⁡{Zij}1+exp⁡{Zij}. Z_{ij}=\ln\left(\frac{Y_{ij}}{k_j-Y_{ij}}\right) \Longleftrightarrow Y_{ij}=\frac{k_j\exp\{Z_{ij}\}}{1+\exp\{Z_{ij}\}}. \tag{1}

Applying the transformation presented in Equation (1) on the original integration function of Samejima (1973), the probability density function of ZijZ_{ij} is defined by:

fZ(zij∣θi,aj,bj,αj)=aj2παj2exp⁡{−[aj(θi−bj−zijαj)]22}. f_Z(z_{ij}\mid\theta_i,a_j,b_j,\alpha_j)= \frac{a_j}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha_j^2}} \exp\left\{-\frac{\left[a_j\left(\theta_i-b_j-\frac{z_{ij}}{\alpha_j}\right)\right]^2}{2}\right\}. \tag{2}

where θi\theta_i is the latent trait of the ith individual, θi∈ℝ\theta_i \in \mathbb{R}, ∀i=1,2,…,n\forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n. aja_j, bjb_j and αj\alpha_j are the IRT parameters, representing respectively the discrimination, difficulty and scale referring to the j-th item, such as aj>0a_j > 0, bj∈ℝb_j \in \mathbb{R}, αj>0\alpha_j > 0, ∀j=1,2,…,p\forall j = 1, 2, \ldots, p. When analyzing the structure of Equation (2), it is easy to see that Zij∣θi,aj,bj,αjZ_{ij}\mid\theta_i,a_j,b_j,\alpha_j has the following probability distribution:

Zij∣θi,aj,bj,αj∼N(αj(θi−bj),αj2aj2). Z_{ij}\mid\theta_i,a_j,b_j,\alpha_j \sim N\left(\alpha_j(\theta_i-b_j),\frac{\alpha_j^2}{a_j^2}\right). \tag{3}

To implement the adjustment of any IRT model for the estimation of a single latent trait, the test must be unidimensional, that is, the observed responses must largely be measuring a single latent variable (Fox, 2010). This assumption can be verified by a correlation analysis between the responses to items two by two. What is desired is that the correlations are all positive and statistically different from zero.

Additionally, item responses need to be independent when conditioned on the latent trait. As seen by Hambleton et al. (1991), unidimensionality implies local independence, so checking the first assumption already guarantees that the second is also true. Finally, there is a third assumption that the latent trait is distributed following a standard Normal distribution. Although applied IRT studies are based on this assumption, it is possible to adjust IRT models considering non-normal distributions for the latent trait (Zhang et al., 2021).

2.1.1 Interpretation of parameters

The interpretation of CRM parameters can be done using the cumulative characteristic curves. Following the formulation of Samejima (1969) and its extension for continuous responses (Samejima, 1973), for a cutoff point λ\lambda, such as 0<λ<kj0 < \lambda < k_j, it is defined:

zjλ=ln⁡(λkj−λ), z_{j\lambda}=\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{k_j-\lambda}\right),

and, from the reparameterization of Wang and Zeng (1998), the cumulative probability of the individual ii presenting an answer in item jj equal to or greater than λ\lambda is:

P(Yij≥λ∣θi)=Φ[aj(θi−bj−1αjln(λkj−λ))]. P(Y_{ij}\geq\lambda\mid\theta_i)= \Phi\left[a_j\left(\theta_i-b_j- \frac{1}{\alpha_j}\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{k_j-\lambda}\right)\right)\right].

The parameter aja_j controls the slope of the curves and therefore the item’s ability to discriminate. High values ​​of aja_j produce steeper transitions between low and high probabilities, more accurately distinguishing individuals with levels close to θ\theta. Reduced values ​​produce flatter curves and noisier responses relative to the latent trait. In normal CRM, the information provided by the item is constant throughout the scale and corresponds to:

Ij(θ)=aj2. I_j(\theta)=a_j^2.

Consequently, the total information from a test with pp items is additive, so that:

I(θ)=∑j=1pIj(θ)=∑j=1paj2. I(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}I_j(\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}a_j^2.

Thus, items with greater discrimination provide more information and reduce the standard error associated with trait estimation. Figure 2 illustrates this behavior by keeping bjb_j and αj\alpha_j fixed.

crm_p_ge_lambda = function(theta, lambda, a, b, alpha, k){
  v = a * (theta - b - (1 / alpha) * log(lambda / (k - lambda)))
  pnorm(v)
}

build_crm_data = function(theta, a_values, b_values, alpha_values,
                            k = 5, lambda_values = 1:4){

  stopifnot(length(a_values) == length(b_values))
  stopifnot(length(a_values) == length(alpha_values))
  stopifnot(all(lambda_values > 0))
  stopifnot(all(lambda_values < k))

  n_items = length(a_values)

  curve_data = expand.grid(
    theta = theta,
    item = 1:n_items,
    lambda = lambda_values
  ) %>%
    mutate(
      a = a_values[item],
      b = b_values[item],
      alpha = alpha_values[item],

      P_ge_lambda = crm_p_ge_lambda(
        theta = theta,
        lambda = lambda,
        a = a,
        b = b,
        alpha = alpha,
        k = k
      ),

      lambda_factor = factor(
        lambda,
        levels = lambda_values
      )
    )

  return(curve_data)
}

plot_item_crm = function(i, curve_data){

  df_prob = curve_data %>%

2.2 Logistic Regression

Logistic Regression does not directly model the outcome, but rather the conditional probability that the response variable assumes the value 1 (success) given a set of predictors (Cox and Hinkley, 1979). Let WiW_i be the binary distal response for the ith individual (i.e., Wi∈{0,1}W_i \in \{0,1\}, ∀i=1,2,⋯,n\forall i = 1, 2, \cdots, n), Equation (4) describes the logit function, which linearizes the relationship between an observed predictor XX and the binary outcome:

ηi=ln⁡[P(Wi=1∣Xi)1−P(Wi=1∣Xi)]=β0+β1Xi. \eta_i=\ln\left[\frac{P(W_i=1\mid X_i)}{1-P(W_i=1\mid X_i)}\right] =\beta_0+\beta_1X_i. \tag{4}

Likewise, qiq_i is defined as:

qi=11+exp⁡[−(β0+β1Xi)]. q_i=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+\beta_1X_i)]}. \tag{5}

Since qiq_i is a probability (i.e., 0≥qi>10 \geq q_i > 1, ∀i=1,2,…,n\forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n), then the distribution of Wi∣Xi,β0,β1W_i\mid X_i,\beta_0,\beta_1 is given as:

Wi∣Xi,β0,β1∼Bern⁡(qi). W_i\mid X_i,\beta_0,\beta_1 \sim \operatorname{Bern}(q_i). \tag{6}

2.3 Estimation Methods

Considering CRM as a measurement model and logistic regression as structural regression, the estimation process must be capable of estimating the model that is in accordance with the structure presented in Figure 1. To this end, the two-step estimation approach and the joint estimation of IRT and regression will be compared.

2.3.1 Two-Step Estimation

In two-step estimation, the CRM is first adjusted to the transformed continuous responses. The conditional independence of the items allows combining their individual contributions and integrating the latent trait, usually assumed as standard normal, to obtain the marginal likelihood of the discrimination, difficulty and scale parameters (Zhang et al., 2021). As this integration makes direct maximization difficult, the Expectation-Maximization algorithm treats the trace as unobserved data: in the expectation step, it calculates the conditional quantities necessary under the current parameters; in the maximization step, it updates the item parameters. The algebraic breakdown and update rules are presented by Wang and Zeng (1998).

Calibration first estimates the parameters of all items and then the individual traits conditioned by this calibration. To reduce the computational cost of each cycle, Shojima (2005) proposed a non-iterative solution in normal CRM, with explicit forms in the hope step and closed solutions in the maximization step under vague priors. This approach is implemented in the EstCRM package in R (Zopluoglu, 2012). The threshold of 10−410^{-4} was considered as a convergence criterion, with a maximum limit of 1000 iterations per EM cycle.

In the second step, the estimated traits are entered as predictors in a conventional logistic regression. The coefficients are obtained by maximum likelihood and, as the problem does not have a closed analytical solution, the R function glm() employs iteratively reweighted least squares. At each iteration, the procedure updates the linear predictor and probabilities, constructs a pseudo-observed response and weights dependent on the current probabilities, and solves a weighted least squares problem; updates continue until parameters or log-likelihood stabilize (Maalouf, 2011).

This procedure is straightforward, but it treats each estimated latent score as if it were observed without error. Schofield (2008) shows that the measurement uncertainty that accompanies the trait is not incorporated into the regression and can produce attenuation of the coefficient associated with the latent predictor. In other words, the greater the measurement error, the greater the underestimation of the structural effect (Junker et al., 2012; Schofield et al., 2015). The factor score regression literature documents similar behavior when predicted scores are used as covariates without correction (Skrondal and Laake, 2001; Bolck et al., 2004; Devlieger et al., 2016).

2.3.2 Joint Estimation

In joint estimation, CRM and logistic regression form a single hierarchical model. The transformed responses inform the item parameters and individual traits, while the binary outcome is directly conditioned on the same latent trait. Thus, the uncertainty about each θi\theta_i is propagated to the estimation of the regression coefficients, instead of being discarded between two steps (Fox and Glas, 2003; Junker et al., 2012).

The inference is Bayesian: the joint posterior distribution conditioned on the observed data combines the likelihood of the measurement model, the likelihood of the outcome and the prior distributions (Dempster, 1968). Vague normal priors were used for item parameters and regression coefficients, with positive truncation for discrimination and scale, and a standard normal prior for the latent trait. The formulation follows the perspective of estimating IRT models through Bayesian simulation discussed by Fox (2010), Kim and Bolt (2007) and Patz and Junker (1999).

As the posterior distribution does not have a simple analytical form, sampling is done using Monte Carlo Methods via Markov Chains. JAGS implements conditional updates and, when necessary, Metropolis-Hastings mechanisms, building chains whose stationary distribution is the posterior of interest (Metropolis et al., 1953; Hastings, 1970; Jackman, 2000; Plummer, 2017). The implementation uses the rjags interface (Plummer et al., 2025), and chain diagnostics and summaries are supported by the coda package (Plummer et al., 2006).

2.4.2 Generation of the trace, CRM items and outcome

For each replication, the vector parameters are expanded into n×pn\times p matrices. The conditional mean of each transformed response is alpha * (theta - b) and its standard deviation is alpha / a, exactly as in Equation (3). After generating Z, the inverse logistic transformation produces the continuous responses Y in the range (0,1)(0,1). The recording lines appear commented in the original files; Therefore, this report does not rewrite or replace existing simulated data.

Z = matrix(NA, n, p)
Y = matrix(NA, n, p)

for (m in 1:r) {
  set.seed(229182 + m)
  theta_mat = matrix(theta, n, p)
  b_mat = matrix(b, n, p, byrow = TRUE)
  a_mat = matrix(a, n, p, byrow = TRUE)
  alpha_mat = matrix(alpha, n, p, byrow = TRUE)

  mu = alpha_mat * (theta_mat - b_mat)
  sd = alpha_mat / a_mat
  Z = matrix(rnorm(n * p, mean = as.vector(mu),
                   sd = as.vector(sd)), n, p)
  Y = k * exp(Z) / (1 + exp(Z))
  results = Y
}

The distal outcome uses the same true trait as a predictor. The logit is calculated with the fixed values ​​of β0\beta_0 and β1\beta_1, converted to probability and used in generating a Bernoulli response for each individual and replication. The seed changes with m, maintaining distinct and reproducible replications.

beta_0 = 0.2
beta_1 = 1.5
x = true_thetas[[4]]

2.4.3 Two-step estimation

In the first step, each continuous response matrix is ​​provided to EstCRMitem. The argument type="Shojima" selects the update proposed by Shojima, BFGS=TRUE enables complementary numerical optimization, converge=0.0001 defines the tolerance and max.EMCycle=1000 imposes the cycle limit. Estimated parameters are saved per item and replication. Then, EstCRMperson calculates the individual scores conditioned on calibration, using the second column of fit_theta$thetas.

theta_estimates_EM = matrix(0, n, r)
a_estimates_EM = matrix(0, p, r)
b_estimates_EM = matrix(0, p, r)
alpha_estimates_EM = matrix(0, p, r)

for (m in 1:r) {
  Y = as.data.frame(read.table(
    file.path(irt_data_dir, paste0("IRTData_", m, ".txt"))))

  irt_fit = EstCRM::EstCRMitem(
    Y, max.item = rep(k, p), min.item = rep(0, p),
    max.EMCycle = 1000, converge = 0.0001,
    type = "Shojima", BFGS = TRUE
  )

  a_estimates_EM[, m] = irt_fit$param[, 1]
  b_estimates_EM[, m] = irt_fit$param[, 2]
  alpha_estimates_EM[, m] = irt_fit$param[, 3]

  theta_fit = EstCRM::EstCRMperson(
    Y, ipar = as.matrix(irt_fit$param),
    max.item = rep(k, p), min.item = rep(0, p)
  )
  theta_estimates_EM[, m] = theta_fit$thetas[, 2]
}

In the second stage, the outcome of each replication is associated with the respective vector of scores estimated by a binomial regression with logit link. The intercept and coefficient of theta_hat are stored for later empirical evaluation.

beta_0_estimates_EM = rep(0, r)
beta_1_estimates_EM = rep(0, r)

for (m in 1:r) {
  W = read.table(file.path(
    regression_data_dir, paste0("LogisticRegressionData_", m, ".txt")))$V1
  theta_hat = theta_estimates_EM[, m]
  fit_logit = glm(W ~ theta_hat,
                  family = binomial(link = "logit"))
  beta_0_estimates_EM[m] = coef(fit_logit)[1]
  beta_1_estimates_EM[m] = coef(fit_logit)[2]
}

2.4.4 Joint estimation

The JAGS model reproduces the CRM normal distribution for Z, associates W directly with the latent trait by a logistic regression, and assigns a standard normal to each theta. The priors of a and alpha are normals truncated at zero; those of b, beta_0 and beta_1 are normal vacancies. The initial values ​​are the same in the three chains in the original code: discriminations and scales start at 1, and the other parameters at zero.

model_specification = "
model {
  for (i in 1:n) {
    for (j in 1:p) {
      Z[i, j] ~ dnorm(alpha[j]*(theta[i] - b[j]), tau[i, j])
      tau[i, j] <- pow(alpha[j]/a[j], -2)
    }
    W[i] ~ dbin((ilogit(beta_0 + beta_1*theta[i])), 1)
    theta[i] ~ dnorm(0.0, 1.0)
  }
  for (j in 1:p) {
    a[j] ~ dnorm(1.0, 0.001) T(0, )
    b[j] ~ dnorm(0.0, 0.001)
    alpha[j] ~ dnorm(1.0, 0.001) T(0, )
  }
  beta_0 ~ dnorm(0.0, 1.0E-06)
  beta_1 ~ dnorm(0.0, 1.0E-06)
}
"

writeLines(model_specification, con = "modelsimCRMreglognormal.txt")
jags.inits = function(chain) {
  list(a = rep(1, p), b = rep(0, p), alpha = rep(1, p),
       theta = rep(0, n), beta_0 = 0, beta_1 = 0)
}
params.jags = c("a", "b", "alpha", "theta", "beta_0", "beta_1")
nchains = 3

In each replication, Y is reconverted to Z, forming with W, n and p the data list. JAGS runs 2000 adaptation iterations, discards another 2000 as burn-in, and retains 4000 per chain, without thinning. The chains are assembled into a matrix; the posterior average of each parameter provides the saved point estimate for that replication.

for (m in 1:r) {
  set.seed(99829 + m)
  Y = read.table(file.path(
    irt_data_dir, paste0("IRTData_", m, ".txt")))
  Z = log(Y / (k - Y))
  W = read.table(file.path(
    regression_data_dir, paste0("LogisticRegressionData_", m, ".txt")))$V1
  data_list = list(Z = Z, W = W, n = n, p = p)

  jags_model = rjags::jags.model(
    file = "modelsimCRMreglognormal.txt",
    data = data_list,
    inits = function() jags.inits(chain = 1:nchains),
    n.chains = nchains, n.adapt = 2000
  )
  update(jags_model, n.iter = 2000)
  samples = coda::coda.samples(
    model = jags_model, variable.names = params.jags,
    n.iter = 4000, thin = 1
  )

  sims_list = as.matrix(samples)
  theta_estimates_mean[, m] = apply(
    sims_list[, grep("^theta", colnames(sims_list))], 2, mean)
  a_estimates_mean[, m] = apply(
    sims_list[, grep("^a\\[", colnames(sims_list))], 2, mean)
  b_estimates_mean[, m] = apply(
    sims_list[, grep("^b\\[", colnames(sims_list))], 2, mean)
  alpha_estimates_mean[, m] = apply(
    sims_list[, grep("^alpha\\[", colnames(sims_list))], 2, mean)
  beta_0_estimates_mean[m] = mean(sims_list[, "beta_0"])
  beta_1_estimates_mean[m] = mean(sims_list[, "beta_1"])

3 Numerical Results

The purpose of the numerical evaluation was to compare the performance of the two-step frequentist estimation and the joint Bayesian estimation as discussed in section 2. Figure 6 indicates a different behavior between the two methods throughout the sampling scenarios.

Figure 6: Comparison of β1\beta_1 estimates in relation to the true value (dashed line) for each simulated scenario tested.

Source: author’s own creation.

In smaller sample sizes (n=100n = 100 and n=250n = 250), the joint Bayesian estimation presents greater dispersion and tends to overestimate β1\beta_1. This result suggests that, in small samples, the joint estimation of item parameters, individual latent traits, and regression parameters may produce additional instability. As the joint model propagates the uncertainty of θ\theta directly to the structural regression, the estimation of β1\beta_1 depends heavily on the joint identification of the latent scale, the item parameters and the relationship with the outcome.

Interestingly, for these same sample sizes, the estimates of β1\beta_1 by the two-step estimation method are more concentrated around the true value when compared to the joint method. On the other hand, as the sample size increases (n=500n = 500 and n=1000n = 1000), there is a tendency to underestimate the effect of the latent trait on the distal response, which was pointed out in section 1.

On the other hand, for larger samples (n=500n = 500 and n=1000n = 1000), there is a significant improvement in the joint estimation behavior, whose β1\beta_1 estimates are concentrated closer to the true value with a clear reduction in dispersion between replications. This pattern is compatible with the expectation that joint estimation benefits from increased sample information, as larger samples provide more evidence both for identifying the latent trait and for estimating the structural association between θ\theta and WW.

Furthermore, Table 1 evaluates the quality of item parameter estimators in both methods tested in the study with simulated data.

Analysis of the Table 1 results concludes that there were no substantial changes in the bias or RMSE of item parameter estimates when both methods are compared. This is an important conclusion, as it allows us to infer that the joint estimation of CRM and regression does not affect the estimation of item parameters. In this way, one of the great advantages of using IRT remains preserved, which is the interpretation of each of the items in a test through their discrimination, difficulty or scale parameters.

Figure 7 shows that, in all scenarios, the average of θ̂i\widehat{\theta}_i aligns very closely with their respective true values. The point cloud follows the identity line, suggesting that both the two-step method and the joint method preserve well the ordering of individuals in the latent trait. On the other hand, the main difference between the methods is not the mean, but the dispersion. In the two-step method, the standard deviation of θ̂i\widehat{\theta}_i is practically equal to 1 in all simulated sample sizes. However, the standard deviations are smaller: 0.918, 0.957, 0.968 and 0.974, respectively. This suggests a contraction of θ̂i\widehat{\theta}_i in the joint method, that is, the estimated values ​​tend to be a little more concentrated around zero.

When it comes to how latent trait estimation affects the behavior of β̂1\widehat{\beta}_1, in smaller scenarios, especially n=100n = 100 and n=250n = 250, the joint method showed greater dispersion and a tendency to overestimate β1\beta_1. This may be related to the fact that, in these scenarios, the thetas estimated by the joint method are more compressed in relation to the true scale. When the latent variable used in the regression has less variability, the coefficient associated with it can increase to represent the same relationship with the outcome, contributing to higher β1\beta_1 estimates. Additionally, in small samples, the joint estimation of the latent variable, item parameters and regression parameters adds instability, which is reflected in the greater dispersion of the boxplots.

4 Conclusion and Future Work

The results presented in section 3 demonstrate that, in fact, treating a latent variable as observed results in the underestimation of its effect on another directly measured variable. This can be verified in the analysis of Figure 6, especially in larger sample sizes. As a solution, the joint estimation of IRT and regression seems to mitigate the β̂1\widehat{\beta}_1 bias problem, especially considering large samples. Furthermore, as seen in Table 1, there is no significant loss in the estimation of item parameters in the joint estimation method when compared to the two-step estimation. Therefore, it can be said that asymptotically the joint estimation method presents satisfactory properties and tends to be better than the two-step estimation.

However, some problems still need to be studied in relation to the joint estimation method. Firstly, this approach is more unstable in estimating β1\beta_1 when the sample size is smaller, even pointing out the two-step estimation method as more stable and less biased. Second, the joint estimation using the Bayesian method slightly underestimates the dispersion of the θi\theta_i, especially in smaller sample sizes. This may be a factor that explains excessive overestimation of β1\beta_1 by the ensemble method in small samples. Therefore, improvements for joint estimation must be studied, especially in small samples.

A possible solution is to adopt a system of hierarchical priors for CRM, as already tested in other IRT models (Kim and Bolt, 2007; Natesan et al., 2016). By adopting priors for the mean and precision hyperparameters of the latent trait and item parameters it may be interesting to correct the problem of underestimation or overestimation of extreme θi\theta_i. Therefore, this can reduce the instability of the estimation of β1\beta_1 in joint estimation, which can produce similar results, or even better, than the two-step estimation.

Another aspect to be observed is whether the magnitude of the bias of β̂1\widehat{\beta}_1 is changed according to the value of β1\beta_1. That is, if the latent trait has a greater effect on the distal response, it may be that the estimates of this effect in the joint method may become more unstable as the true value of β1\beta_1 is greater. Likewise, it may be that the underestimation of β1\beta_1 is also of greater magnitude when estimating the two-step model considering larger β1\beta_1.

Therefore, future work to be proposed includes the joint estimation of CRM and regression using other prior systems, especially hierarchical priors and with different values ​​of β1\beta_1. It is also important to highlight the importance of using more recent statistical tools with better computational power, as is the case with stan (Gabry et al., 2025). Other issues may be considered in the future, such as the presence of missing data, non-normal distributions, presence of covariates in the IRT, among other aspects.
